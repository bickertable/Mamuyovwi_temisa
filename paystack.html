Principle Four: Trust Is Compounded, Not Purchased

In October 2020, Stripe announced it was acquiring Paystack for a reported $200 million. From outside the ecosystem it looked sudden. Inside it, the acquisition was not where the story began. It was where years of quiet choices finally surfaced.

Shola Akinlade and Ezra Olubi met studying Computer Science at Babcock University. Shola had built Precurio, an enterprise collaboration platform adopted across Africa, through which he learned that even fully operational businesses struggled to get paid online. Ezra had worked at Jobberman, Delivery Science and Softcom, where he saw first-hand how fragile African financial infrastructure was. By the early 2010s both had independently concluded that payments were the biggest bottleneck to digital business in Nigeria.

One question, held onto

Paystack was conceived as a developer-first payments company, and the discipline was in what it refused to do. Other fintechs and banks had launched online payment products before 2015. Most were difficult to integrate, poorly documented, and minimally supported.

Rather than solving every financial problem at once, Shola and Ezra concentrated on one question: how could businesses get paid online, reliably, every time? That focus shaped the product design, the support model, and eventually the acquisition.

Their first Y Combinator application was rejected. They treated it as diagnostic rather than final, refined the pitch around evidence of traction from Nigerian merchants, and were accepted into the Winter 2016 batch as the first Nigerian fintech in the programme. The foundation had already been laid in Lagos. YC was a springboard, not a starting line.

What compounding actually looks like

Before public launch, roughly 300 businesses joined a waitlist. Shola and Ezra personally walked early adopters through integration, gathered feedback, and iterated on documentation, error handling and support workflows. Early customers included Gingerbox, Jekalo and DIYLaw.

Paystack launched publicly in January 2016 with a striking promise: a merchant could begin accepting payments online in as little as 30 minutes, against the weeks or months legacy systems required.

None of that is a single dramatic move. It is the same small act performed thousands of times. Clean APIs. Clear documentation. Error messages that told a developer what had actually gone wrong. Responsive support on an ordinary Tuesday. By 2020 the company served over 60,000 businesses across e-commerce, telecommunications, retail, logistics and betting.

Every interaction with a customer is a deposit into trust or a withdrawal from it. Companies that treat support as a cost centre are making withdrawals they will not notice until the account is empty.

Why Stripe bought rather than built

Stripe had led Paystack's $8 million Series A in 2018 and knew the team well. Entering Africa directly meant confronting fragmented banking infrastructure, regulatory complexity, and market-specific behaviour. Stripe acquired a company that had already solved those problems at scale.

What transferred in the deal was not primarily code. It was merchant trust, local expertise, and operational knowledge, none of which Stripe could have purchased separately or built quickly.

Paystack continued operating independently from Lagos under its existing leadership, later expanding into South Africa, Kenya and Côte d'Ivoire.

The transferable lesson

Subsidy buys users. Only reliability buys retention. A merchant who trusts a payments provider does not leave for a cheaper alternative, because the switching cost is not the fee difference. It is the cost of rebuilding trust in an untested provider.

That is a moat you cannot raise a round to build. You can only accumulate it.
