Moniepoint · Chapter 1

Every layer paid for the one above it

Principle Five: Sequence Your Growth or It Will Sequence You

Tosin Eniolorunda joined Interswitch in 2009 as a software engineer and spent six years moving through roles that gave him an unusually complete view of Nigerian payments. He programmed the first point-of-sale software for Interswitch, which became foundational for the majority of POS terminals in the country.

He left with the thing that mattered most, and it was not capital. He had not saved enough. He lacked the network to open doors or unlock funding. What he had was a precise understanding of how hard it was for ordinary Nigerians to transact with their own money, and where in the stack that difficulty actually sat.

The build order

Moniepoint did not try to sell credit to merchants on day one. It built in a sequence where each layer created the conditions for the next.

Payment rails first. The unglamorous processing layer that everything else would run on.

Then agent distribution. A physical network reaching corners of Nigeria that no bank branch served, which is expensive, slow, and nearly impossible for a competitor to replicate with capital alone.

Then merchant tools. Products that only made sense once the merchants were already transacting on the rails.

Then credit. The highest-margin product, launched last.

Why credit had to come last

By the time Moniepoint launched credit products, it held transaction data on hundreds of thousands of merchants. Its risk models were built on observed behaviour rather than formal financial history, which most of these merchants did not have.

A competitor entering the credit market at the same moment had access to the same capital markets and the same engineering talent. What it did not have was the history. That gap could not be closed by raising more money, because the data was a by-product of years of operating the layers underneath.

Moniepoint's advantage in credit was not a funding advantage. It was a sequencing advantage, and sequencing advantages cannot be bought.

The contrast case

OPay's super-app experiment attempted ride-hailing, food delivery, logistics, lending and payments simultaneously. That failure is usually described as over-diversification. It was not. Diversification was not the mistake. Sequencing was. The payments foundation was not stable enough, the trust not deep enough, and the operational infrastructure not robust enough to carry five distinct businesses at once. When the Lagos motorcycle ban and the pandemic forced a reckoning, OPay retreated to its core, which turned out to be what it should have been building all along.

What the sequence produced

By the end of 2023, Moniepoint was processing around 800 million transactions a month with values exceeding $17 billion monthly, serving over 10 million customers. In October 2024 it raised $110 million in a Series C led by Development Partners International, with participation from Google's Africa Investment Fund and Verod Capital, taking its valuation above $1 billion.

In the same year, the Financial Times and Statista ranked it the second fastest-growing company in Africa.

The transferable lesson

Sequencing is the most practically important strategic question of a company's first five years, and most founders answer a different question instead. They ask what would be most valuable if they could build everything at once. The useful question is what, built first, makes everything after it more achievable and more defensible.

Get the sequence right and you create flywheels that compound. Get it wrong and you create complexity that compounds, which is a very different thing.
