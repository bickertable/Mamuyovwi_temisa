M-Pesa · Chapter 12

The product survived because the regulator was persuaded, not defeated

M-Pesa's first founding moment was technical. Its second was political, and the second one was harder.

Principle Two: The Regulator Is Your Most Important Partner

M-Pesa was designed to solve one narrow problem: helping microfinance borrowers in Kenya repay loans through their Safaricom phones. Nick Hughes, an innovation manager at Vodafone, had asked a question bankers were not asking. What if repayments could happen over the phone? Vodafone secured a £1 million grant from the UK Department for International Development to find out.

The 2005 pilot ran with a microfinance institution called Faulu Kenya in Kibera, Nairobi. Its borrowers were women running small businesses: tailors, vegetable sellers, charcoal traders. Exactly the customers traditional banks had written off.

The users redesigned the product

They did not just repay loans. They sent money to each other. They paid school fees. They stored savings in a digital account a controlling husband could not easily reach. They took payments from customers across town without carrying cash on dangerous roads.

Hughes and his colleague Susie Lonie watched this and made the decision that turned a pilot into infrastructure: expand the scope. If people were already treating M-Pesa as a money transfer service, make it one. They rebuilt the product around peer-to-peer transfers and recruited local shopkeepers, pharmacists and traders as cash-in, cash-out agents.

The elegance was in the reuse. Safaricom already had national distribution through airtime resellers. Every corner shop selling scratch cards could become a de facto bank branch. The infrastructure existed. It needed repurposing, not building.

Then the banks tried to kill it

Growth was immediate. One million users within twelve months. Eight million by 2009, roughly a third of Kenya's adult population. By 2010, M-Pesa was processing more transactions than Western Union handled globally in a year.

In 2008, a coalition of Kenyan banks formally petitioned the Central Bank of Kenya to shut it down. Their argument was that Safaricom was conducting unlicensed banking activity: holding customer deposits, facilitating transfers, operating as a shadow bank without the oversight that comes with the responsibility.

The CBK convened a review. The banks assumed it was a matter of time.

What Michael Joseph did instead of waiting

Safaricom's CEO engaged the Central Bank Governor, Njuguna Ndung'u, directly and repeatedly. His argument was narrow and factual: M-Pesa's funds were held in a trust account at a regulated commercial bank. Every shilling was backed and accounted for. The risk the banks described was already being managed. What the banks actually feared was competition.

The CBK ruled in M-Pesa's favour, concluding it was not a deposit-taking institution in the traditional sense and did not require a full banking licence. It was a payment service. The regulation would be built around the product rather than forcing the product to conform to rules written for a world that no longer existed.

The regulator who understands your product is your defender. The regulator who does not understand it is your existential risk.

What travelled and what did not

By 2012, transactions worth more than a quarter of Kenya's GDP were passing through M-Pesa annually. A 2016 study published in Science found that access to M-Pesa had lifted roughly 194,000 Kenyan households out of extreme poverty, with the effect concentrated among female-headed households.

International expansion was more instructive than successful. M-Pesa worked in Tanzania. It struggled in South Africa, where banks were well-capitalised and politically connected, and in India, where state-backed payment systems were reshaping the market.

The lesson is often misread as evidence that African solutions do not travel. That is not it. M-Pesa travelled badly to markets that did not share the structural conditions that made it inevitable in Kenya, and travelled well to markets that did. Solutions built for specific structural conditions move to markets with the same conditions, which exist in abundance across the developing world.

The transferable lesson

Most companies treat regulatory affairs as a compliance function that activates when something goes wrong. M-Pesa survived because Safaricom treated it as a strategic function, invested in it continuously, and had a relationship in place before it needed one.

Hire that capability before you need it. When regulators push back, do not fight. Educate.
