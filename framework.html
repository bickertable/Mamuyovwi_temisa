The framework

The Seven Principles of the 100-Year Company

Drawn from the closing chapter of From the Ground Up. Seven patterns shared by every company in the book, whatever their market, model, or founding decade. This is the framework I use with advisory clients.

  1. Principle One

    Infrastructure Before Products

    Every enduring company in the book built infrastructure before it built consumer products. Interswitch built the switching network before Verve cards or Quickteller. M-Pesa built the agent network before the financial products that ran on top of it. Moniepoint built the agent banking rails before the business banking products that generated its highest margins.

    A consumer product can be copied in months by a well-funded competitor. A physical agent network covering all 36 Nigerian states cannot. The founders who succeed longest are often the ones who resist monetising early, accepting slower revenue in the foundational years in exchange for a position that makes monetisation at scale nearly inevitable.

    Ask: not what product can I build and sell, but what infrastructure, once built, makes a family of products possible that no one else can build?

  2. Principle Two

    The Regulator Is Your Most Important Partner

    Every significant company in the book had a moment where its relationship with a regulator determined whether it survived. Interswitch needed the Central Bank of Nigeria to accept that a private switching company could become national payment infrastructure. M-Pesa needed the Central Bank of Kenya to rule it was not a deposit-taking institution. Wave needed the BCEAO to grant an EMI licence that had never gone to a non-bank, non-telecom entity.

    In each case the relationship was managed proactively, as a strategic function, not reactively as compliance. The African regulatory environment is not an obstacle course to be navigated with minimal contact. It is a relationship ecosystem to be cultivated with sustained investment.

    Hire regulatory capability before you need it. Treat regulatory engagement as a product function, not a legal one. When regulators push back, do not fight. Educate.

  3. Principle Three

    Build for the Customer Who Does Not Yet Exist

    The most important customers, at the moment of maximum growth, were rarely the customers these companies set out to serve. M-Pesa was designed for microfinance borrowers repaying loans. Its most transformative use came from women in Nairobi storing savings out of reach of controlling family members. Chipper Cash was designed for cross-border peer-to-peer transfers. Its most valuable segment became diaspora remittances.

    None of these companies predicted the behaviour. They built platforms general enough that customers could use them for unanticipated purposes, and they had the agility to follow those customers rather than insist on the original use case.

    Products designed around rigid use cases produce rigid outcomes. Design around fundamental human needs instead: store value securely, move money reliably, access services without judgement.

  4. Principle Four

    Trust Is Compounded, Not Purchased

    The most common mistake well-funded companies make is attempting to buy trust with subsidy. OPay's zero-fee model acquired users efficiently, but users who joined because transfers were free did not necessarily stay because they trusted OPay. PalmPay's cashback growth created a large base, and converting incentive-driven behaviour into necessity-driven usage took years.

    The companies that built durable trust did it by compounding. Paystack made documentation excellent and error messages clear, thousands of times, across thousands of integrations. Moniepoint became the payments partner that worked during cash crises and outages that paralysed competitors.

    Every customer interaction is a deposit into trust or a withdrawal from it. Companies that treat support as a cost centre are making withdrawals they will not notice until the account is empty.

  5. Principle Five

    Sequence Your Growth or It Will Sequence You

    The most expensive mistakes in African tech were made by companies doing too many things at once without the structural foundation to support them. OPay's super-app experiment was not wrong because diversification is wrong. It was wrong in its sequencing. The payments foundation was not stable enough to carry five distinct businesses.

    Moniepoint is the instructive contrast. Payment rails, then agent distribution, then merchant tools, then credit. Each layer built on the stability and data advantage of the layers before it. By the time credit launched, its risk models had transaction history no competitor could match.

    Sequencing is the most practically important strategic question of a company's first five years. Not what would be most valuable if you could build everything at once, but what, built first, makes everything after it more achievable and more defensible.

  6. Principle Six

    Capital Is a Tool, Not a Strategy

    The funding winter of 2023 and 2024 performed an uncomfortable service. It revealed which companies had been using capital as a strategy and which had been using it as a tool.

    Companies using capital as a strategy believe growth solves all problems. In most African tech businesses the unit economics of serving the next customer are not materially better than the current one, so growth does not fix a broken model. It makes the model larger and more broken. TymeBank did not reach profitability by becoming very large. It reached profitability by designing a model where the cost of acquiring and serving each customer was structurally lower than the revenue that customer generated.

    Capital-efficient businesses with demonstrated unit economics do not just raise more easily in this environment. They are inherently more resilient to the next cycle.

  7. Principle Seven

    Your Hardest Constraint Is Your Greatest Asset

    Every company in the book built something specific because it operated in Africa, not in spite of it. Interswitch built the first African fintech unicorn because of Nigeria's fragmented, cash-dependent banking system. That system created an acute need and the difficulty of serving it became a moat no external competitor could easily cross. JUMO built a credit infrastructure business because most of its customers had no formal credit history, and solving that created a proprietary data asset no traditional lender could replicate.

    The pattern is consistent. The constraint is the opportunity. The question is whether a founder has the clarity to see it that way and the discipline to build for those conditions rather than import a solution from a context where the constraint does not exist.

    The companies most likely to displace you are not the well-funded global platforms with the best technology. They are the local companies with the deepest understanding of your market's constraints.

What the principles add up to

Africa is not a difficult market to build in despite its size, its diversity, its infrastructure gaps and its regulatory complexity. It is the most important market in the world to build in because of those things.

Every one of those constraints is a problem someone will solve. Every problem solved creates a business. Every business that succeeds in Africa creates a template that travels to every other market sharing those structural conditions.

The founders in this book did not build for Africa. They built in Africa, and Africa made them world-class.
