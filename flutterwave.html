Flutterwave · Chapter 5

One integration bought the credibility everything else was built on

In payments, nobody hands you their transactions on the strength of a pitch. Flutterwave needed proof, and Uber gave it to them.

Principle Four: Trust Is Compounded, Not Purchased

Flutterwave began with a problem Iyinoluwa Aboyeji could not stop seeing. At Andela, African software engineers were working for global companies and struggling to get paid, because cross-border payment systems were outdated, broken, or simply unavailable.

He had already learned the lesson that made him take it seriously. After his first company, Bookneto, was acquired in Canada in 2013, Aboyeji asked himself whether he actually understood the problems he was solving there. The systems already worked. Someone else had built them. If he wanted to build something meaningful, he needed to be where the rails did not exist.

Flutterwave was formally co-founded on 2 May 2016 by Aboyeji, Gbenga Agboola, and Adeleke Adekoya. Aboyeji brought scaling experience and investor networks. Agboola, who had worked at PayPal, Google and Access Bank, brought the technical architecture. Adekoya, a banking and payments specialist, brought the ground-level knowledge of local payment flows, compliance requirements and merchant onboarding that made the vision operable.

The credibility problem

In payments, the constraint is not technical. It is trust. No bank, merchant or investor was going to route transactions through a startup without proof it could deliver securely and at scale. And you cannot demonstrate scale without volume, which nobody gives you until you have demonstrated scale.

How they broke the loop

Uber Nigeria, in 2016, had a specific and painful problem. Local banks frequently blocked international card transactions, and drivers went unpaid for days. Flutterwave's API solved it, enabling Uber to accept local and international cards.

The deal did not just bring in a major client. It validated the technology in front of exactly the audience that had been unwilling to listen: banks, merchants, and investors. Airlines, e-commerce platforms and multinationals expanding into Africa followed.

Flutterwave did not win the Uber contract because it had scale. It got scale because it had won the Uber contract.

By the end of 2017, Flutterwave had processed over $1.2 billion across more than 10 million transactions and completed Y Combinator.

Widening the base

In 2018, Rave let small and medium African businesses accept payments from customers anywhere, extending Flutterwave beyond large corporate clients. In 2019, Barter, launched with Visa, moved the company into consumer payments with transfers, virtual cards and bill payment. That year Flutterwave processed $5.4 billion across more than 100 million transactions.

Aboyeji stepped down as CEO in 2019 and Agboola took over, focusing on technical and operational scaling. By 2021 the company had processed over $9 billion, raised a Series C led by Tiger Global and Avenir Growth, and crossed $1 billion in valuation. A 2022 Series D pushed it past $3 billion, funding a US expansion built on state-by-state money transmission licences.

The transferable lesson

Founders in infrastructure businesses often try to solve the credibility problem with positioning: better messaging, a stronger deck, a more impressive advisory board. It rarely works, because the people you need to convince have seen all of it before.

What works is a reference customer with a problem specific enough that solving it proves something no pitch can. Find the client whose pain is acute, public, and adjacent to the audience you actually need. Then solve their problem completely.
