Licensed, not permitted

Principle Two: The Regulator Is Your Most Important Partner

Wave started in Senegal, which was a deliberate choice and a contrarian one. The capital, the press attention and the investor interest were in Lagos and Nairobi. Francophone West Africa was treated as a footnote.

Drew Durbin and Lincoln Quirk had already built Sendwave, a remittance app that gained traction across the African diaspora. That business taught them two things that shaped Wave. Friction in money movement was solvable. And reliable payouts depended on local networks, trusted partners, careful cash management, and strict multi-country compliance.

Cross-border remittance was the smaller problem. Domestic money movement was the larger one. Mobile money in the region was fragmented, expensive, and unreliable.

Growth first, hostility second

Wave launched in Senegal in 2018 and adoption was fast, driven by transactions that were cheaper and simpler than the incumbent alternatives. The agent network grew quickly, and so did the operational strain. Agents ran out of cash and e-float, leaving customers stranded.

By 2019, Wave was growing fast enough to attract the wrong kind of attention. Industry insiders say Orange, the region's dominant telecom operator, blocked Wave from selling airtime, describing the newcomer's fees as too cheap and a threat to the ecosystem.

The move was designed to kill a young company. It did something else. It exposed the structural problem at the centre of Wave's position: in a region where telcos controlled mobile money, Wave was operating on permission that could be withdrawn.

Building toward autonomy

Through 2020, while attention was elsewhere, Wave rebuilt its internal machinery. Liquidity systems were reconstructed. Agent onboarding became stricter. Compliance structures thickened. That work was not defensive housekeeping. It was the precondition for the thing the founders were actually working toward.

In September 2021, Wave announced a $200 million Series A led by Sequoia Capital, with Founders Fund, Stripe, Ribbit Capital and Partech Africa participating, at a $1.7 billion valuation. It was one of the largest early-stage rounds in African history and made Wave the first unicorn in Francophone Africa. A further $90 million followed in early 2022.

The capital mattered less than what it signalled. Regulators paid closer attention.

The licence

On 14 April 2022, Wave received an E-Money Institution licence from the BCEAO, the central bank of the West African Monetary Union. No non-bank, non-telecom startup had ever been granted that autonomy in the region.

Overnight, Wave went from a company operating on someone else's permission to a regulated financial institution able to build its own rails.

The licence allowed Wave to offer its own financial products and expand across eight countries without relying on telco partnerships. It also placed the company under stricter scrutiny, with ongoing audits, capital requirements and compliance obligations.

The harder phase

Inside Wave, the unicorn moment did not feel like a victory lap. Being roughly 70% cheaper than incumbents was never only a marketing position. It was a financial commitment with consequences, and the licence marked the beginning of proving that the model that made Wave famous could also make it sustainable.

The transferable lesson

There is a difference between operating with permission and operating with a licence, and most founders discover it at the worst possible moment. Permission can be withdrawn by a competitor with better relationships. A licence cannot.

If your business depends on an incumbent's cooperation, you do not have a distribution strategy. You have a distribution risk, and the work of converting one into the other starts years before you need it.
