Interswitch · Chapter 6

The rails came first, and nothing else could have worked

Mitchell Elegbe spent four years building a switching network before Interswitch sold a single consumer product. That patience is the whole story.

Principle One: Infrastructure Before Products

An ATM in Scotland swallowed Mitchell Elegbe's card. That is where Africa's first fintech unicorn begins, and the detail matters more than it looks. Elegbe was not frustrated by the failure itself. He was frustrated by the comparison it forced. If a card could be trapped in a country with advanced banking infrastructure, what was happening in Nigeria, where ATMs were scarce, unreliable, and limited to the few banks that had them, and where customers collected tally numbers to withdraw their own money?

He had already seen the machinery from the inside. During his national youth service at Computer Systems Associates, Elegbe helped connect Nigerian banks to the international SWIFT network. He knew what global financial plumbing looked like. He also knew Nigeria did not have any.

The decision that defined the company

When Elegbe left Telnet in 2002, he did not set out to build a bank or sell cards. He set out to build the missing rails: the switching and transaction processing backbone that would let banks talk to one another.

This required three things at once. Engineering a reliable switch. Convincing conservative banks to plug into it. And finding partners with enough credibility to make a local system look like a national one.

The second was harder than the first. Banks saw no reason to trust an unproven startup with their transaction systems. They worried about security, about competition, about losing customers to rivals on a shared network. Elegbe's answer was structural rather than persuasive: Interswitch would build, own, and operate the infrastructure itself. The banks would carry neither the cost nor the risk. They would plug in.

Accenture came in to validate the technical design and commercial model, and that borrowed credibility opened doors that argument alone had not. One by one, the banks signed on. By 2006, what had started in a small office had quietly become the invisible backbone of Nigerian banking.

Four years before a consumer ever saw the product

Verve, Interswitch's own card scheme, launched in 2009. Quickteller, the consumer payments portal, followed in 2010. That is seven years after founding and roughly four years after the switch was carrying real national volume.

Read that sequence carefully, because the temptation runs the other way. A consumer product generates revenue quickly and is easy to explain to investors. A switching network generates nothing for years and is almost impossible to explain to anyone.

A consumer product can be copied in months by a well-funded competitor. A national switching network that every major bank has already integrated with cannot be copied at all.

Verve was not designed to mimic foreign cards. It was EMV certified, meeting international security standards at a time when magnetic stripe fraud was a growing concern, but it was built around what Nigerians actually needed and priced for the local market. It could only exist because the rails underneath it already did.

What the patience bought

In November 2019, Visa acquired a 20% minority stake, valuing Interswitch at approximately $1 billion and confirming its status as Africa's first fintech unicorn. In 2023, Interswitch became one of the first companies to receive a Payments Service Holding Company licence from the Central Bank of Nigeria, allowing it to hold customer deposits and offer non-payment services.

Neither of those outcomes was available to a company that had started with a consumer app.

The transferable lesson

The question most founders ask is: what product can I build and sell? The question Interswitch answered is: what infrastructure, once built, makes a family of products possible that no one else can build?

The second question produces slower revenue and a much stronger position. If you are choosing between them, you are really choosing between how quickly you want to earn and how long you want to last.
